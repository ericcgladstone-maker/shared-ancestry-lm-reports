# Related-work map and TACL submission constraints

**For Eric, November 1, 2026.** This is a prioritised literature and boundary-of-novelty map for *Can Shared Ancestry Be Recovered from Language-Model Reports?* The task is inference about shared production of *multiple final outputs*, under experimentally controlled LM communication graphs. Sources are verified against publisher/ACL Anthology/arXiv primary pages where available. A cited paper's reported finding is distinguished from what the current study could claim. Publication and preprint status are explicitly noted where material.

## Argumentative placement

A TACL introduction should not claim that detecting shared textual origins is new. Corpus-scale reuse detection, stemmatics, source-sentence provenance, and model attribution precede this design. The defensible novelty claim has four jointly necessary qualifiers: **known experimentally manipulated output genealogies**, **no upstream source texts available to the text-only predictor**, **no production-time watermark or agent log provided**, and **explicit incremental comparison of cross-message relations with a pooled single-message-feature baseline**. Each qualifier should be visible in the Related Work section and in the opening Methods schematic. Whether this combination is new relative to the closest 2026 agent-provenance papers should be stated cautiously, not as a universal priority claim.

### A. Text reuse, source tracing, and provenance

**Zhu et al. (2025), TROVE: A Challenge for Fine-Grained Text Provenance via Source Sentence Tracing and Relationship Classification.** *What it established:* A benchmark and task definition for tracing each sentence in generated target text to specific sentences in available, sometimes lengthy, multi-document input sources, with relation labels such as quotation, compression, and inference. It spans different tasks and languages. *Overlap:* The word “provenance” and attention to text transformations are directly related. *Boundary:* TROVE's system sees a supplied candidate source corpus and seeks local attribution. The present Study 2 text-only classifier sees only terminal reports, not source packages or exact parent texts, and predicts a **relationship among reports**, not a target-to-source sentence link. Do not describe TROVE as solving latent common-root inference. **Priority: essential.** URL: https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.577/

**Smith, Cordell, and Dillon (2013), Infectious Texts: Modeling Text Reuse in Nineteenth-Century Newspapers.** *What it established:* Scalable discovery of overlapping passages using document candidate retrieval, local alignment, and clustering to study reprinting histories. *Overlap:* Shared strings across reports may reveal textual dependence, even if a document lacks explicit metadata. *Boundary:* Reprinting corpora contain identifiable copied passages, often publication dates and broader corpus context. The current objects are freshly generated, paraphrased event reports with deliberately matched single-message assignments, not long historical near-duplicates. Rare shared wording is an adjacent method, not an intrinsically novel concept. **Priority: high.** URL: https://www.khoury.northeastern.edu/home/dasmith/infect-bighum-2013.pdf

**Smith, Cordell, Dillon, Stramp, and Wilkerson (2014), Detecting and Modeling Local Text Reuse.** *What it established:* Efficient discovery of clusters of locally reused passages in large document collections, illustrated by newspaper reprinting and legislative text development. *Overlap:* Pairwise overlap converted to larger dependence structures and the distinction between local lexical reuse and latent circulation patterns. *Boundary:* Matching reused passages and clustering do not identify the direction or full unseen history without additional assumptions. Use this citation to constrain any inference from overlap features to actual ancestry. **Priority: high.** URL: https://doi.org/10.1109/JCDL.2014.6970166

**Smith, Cordell, and Mullen (2015), Computational Methods for Uncovering Reprinted Texts in Antebellum Newspapers.** *What it established:* The Passim-based historical text-reuse method and its application to newspaper reprinting. *Overlap:* Recovery of textual dependence from unannotated documents, and the possibility of repeated passages as evidence of a network. *Boundary:* The copying signal is often more literal and distributed across a large corpus. Pairwise shared language is one of the current paper's feature families, not a unique methodological invention. **Priority: high if historical connections remain in the introduction.** URL: https://doi.org/10.1093/alh/ajv029

### A2. Near-duplicate and plagiarism detection


**Broder, Glassman, Manasse, and Zweig (1997), Syntactic Clustering of the Web.** *What it established:* Introduced a large-scale near-duplicate identification and clustering procedure based on word shingles, document resemblance/containment, and compact sketches. *Overlap:* Pairwise word-sequence sharing as a detectable signature of related documents directly precedes the present model’s rare-sequence overlap feature. *Boundary:* Detecting near-duplicate documents does not identify whether highly similar independently produced reports about identical source facts share a hidden observation ancestor. Its algorithm is designed for efficient clustering, not the experimentally controlled ancestry-versus-common-information contrast. **Priority: high.** URL: https://ftp.zx.net.nz/pub/archive/ftp.digital.com/pub/DEC/SRC/technical-notes/SRC-1997-015.html

**Barrón-Cedeño, Potthast, Rosso, and Stein (2010), Corpus and Evaluation Measures for Automatic Plagiarism Detection.** *What it established:* Introduced evaluation resources and measures for intrinsic and external plagiarism detection, including synthetic plagiarism cases. External detection commonly relies on searching a source corpus, while intrinsic detection tests for changes within a suspect document. *Overlap:* Text reuse can be detected despite transformations, and experimental provenance labels permit quantitative evaluation. *Boundary:* Neither identifying a copied passage against candidate source documents nor detecting a style discontinuity in one document is equivalent to distinguishing hidden shared ancestry among peer outputs whose original observations are unavailable. The study should cite this as a methodological predecessor rather than assert that plagiarism detection lacks any relational components. **Priority: high, but one citation in main text is sufficient.** URL: https://aclanthology.org/L10-1016/

### B. Machine-generated text detection and producer attribution

**Uchendu, Le, Shu, and Lee (2020), Authorship Attribution for Neural Text Generation.** *What it established:* Evaluated attribution of synthetic text to the producing neural language model, using linguistic features and learned approaches. *Overlap:* Generated texts can carry model- or process-associated statistical signals, complicating any attempt to attribute correlation uniquely to common message ancestry. *Boundary:* Producer-model identity is not the same label as whether two outputs share an intermediate observation within one model. The current full-access same-producer design helps control for model identity, although it cannot remove all same-model regularities. **Priority: essential.** URL: https://aclanthology.org/2020.emnlp-main.673/

**Ippolito, Duckworth, Callison-Burch, and Eck (2020), Automatic Detection of Generated Text Is Easiest When Humans Are Fooled.** *What it established:* Machine-generated text detectability depends on sampling strategy and text length, and automatic and human detection can use different cues. *Overlap:* A concrete reason to treat producer decoding, length, and reviewer/classifier comparisons as boundary conditions rather than universal accuracy claims. *Boundary:* Detecting machine text against human text is not estimating a shared-root relationship among outputs of the same producer. **Priority: medium.** URL: https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.164/

**Mitchell, Lee, Khazatsky, Manning, and Finn (2023), DetectGPT: Zero-Shot Machine-Generated Text Detection Using Probability Curvature.** *What it established:* A zero-shot detection criterion based on local curvature of a generator's log-probability surface around a candidate passage. *Overlap:* Demonstrates that unmarked generation can leave measurable distributional cues without trace metadata. *Boundary:* Requires model probability access and discriminates generated-versus-reference text. It does not identify unobserved root sharing, cross-message relationships, or agent topology. **Priority: medium.** URL: https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/mitchell23a.html

**Kirchenbauer, Geiping, Wen, Katz, Miers, and Goldstein (2023), A Watermark for Large Language Models.** *What it established:* Generation-time token selection can deliberately inject statistically detectable signatures that support later identification. *Overlap:* Final text can encode provenance information despite absent explicit metadata. *Boundary:* Watermarking changes generation and uses a known detector/signature. The present output-only design does not insert signatures, so this is an important instrumental contrast. **Priority: high alongside IET.** URL: https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/kirchenbauer23a.html

**Sadasivan, Kumar, Balasubramanian, Wang, and Feizi (2023), Can AI-Generated Text Be Reliably Detected?** *What it established:* Paraphrasing can undermine machine-generation detectors, with an argument linking optimal discrimination to differences between generating distributions. *Overlap:* Cautions against universal claims about persistence of stylometric or wording cues under rewriting or out-of-distribution transfer. *Boundary:* The task is machine-versus-human source identification under potentially adversarial transformations, rather than the randomized dependence structures in the present work. **Priority: medium.** URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11156

### C. Agentic LM systems, source availability, and pipeline provenance

**Nian, Cao, Zhu, Zou, Luan, and Zhao (2026), When Only the Final Text Survives: Implicit Execution Tracing for Multi-Agent Attribution/Auditing.** *What it established:* Implicit Execution Tracing (IET) embeds **agent-specific keyed statistical signals at generation time** and detects them to segment contributions and reconstruct coordination structure from final text. The arXiv abstract displays a title variant for “Attribution” and “Auditing”; check the cited version's PDF title when creating BibTeX. *Overlap:* This is the closest title-level and task-level competitor. Both ask about multi-agent structure from the final artifact. *Boundary:* IET is **provenance by instrumented generation** with keyed detection, while the present study tests uninstrumented, ordinary prompting and the marginal-versus-relational identification contrast. Do not say neither method uses metadata in the same sense: IET embeds metadata-like signals into tokens. **Priority: essential and front-loaded.** URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17445

**Tang, Barke, and Agarwal (2026), Tokengeist: Multi-Turn Attribution Tracing in Agentic Conversations.** *What it established:* A recursive approach to tracing dependence paths backwards through a **known multi-turn conversational context**, with a benchmark for target spans and attributable prior turns. *Overlap:* Provenance over an agentic interaction graph and concern for propagated evidence rather than just direct textual similarity. *Boundary:* The upstream conversation turns are accessible to the attribution method. Here the reader sees multiple endpoints with hidden common roots. The studies have different observability conditions and output targets. **Priority: essential.** URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22610

**Wu et al. (2023), AutoGen: Enabling Next-Gen LLM Applications via Multi-Agent Conversation.** *What it established:* A framework for constructing workflows in which multiple LM agents interact through structured conversations, including human and tool participation. *Overlap:* Supplies a concrete architectural motivation for delegated observation, rewriting, and synthesis. *Boundary:* It is a framework for producing agent conversations with accessible roles/messages, not an output-only statistical test recovering missing provenance. Cite only if the paper needs a systems motivation, not as the main competing inference method. **Priority: optional.** URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08155

### D. Computational stemmatology, contamination, and textual genealogy

**Marmerola, Oikawa, Dias, Goldenstein, and Rocha (2016), On the Reconstruction of Text Phylogeny Trees: Evaluation and Analysis of Textual Relationships.** *What it established:* Evaluated graph/tree reconstruction for near-duplicate text versions, including simulated deletion, insertion, and textual lineage, and discussed stemmatology, missing ancestors, and contamination from multiple exemplars. *Overlap:* Shared innovations, inherited errors, missing intermediates, and mixing from multiple roots are directly germane to IND/DEP/Y designs. *Boundary:* The present study is not unrestricted phylogenetic reconstruction. Its roots are latent, reports share a synthetic world, and outputs can be regenerated independently into similar wording. Direction and unseen internal nodes are not identified by a shared-overlap classifier. **Priority: essential.** URL: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0167822

**Cordell (2015), Reprinting, Circulation, and the Network Author in Antebellum Newspapers.** *What it established:* The social and bibliographic implications of reprints and circulation, cautioning against equating stable named authorship with the production/distribution of circulating texts. *Overlap:* The intuition that seemingly separate texts can depend on copied histories and that textual repetition may evidence networks. *Boundary:* Historical dissemination is not represented by the synthetic agent model. The current design estimates discriminability in a controlled setting rather than the historical route of a passage. **Priority: medium for framing.** URL: https://doi.org/10.1093/alh/ajv028.


**Andrews and Macé (2013), Beyond the Tree of Texts: Building an Empirical Model of Scribal Variation through Graph Analysis of Texts and Stemmata.** *What it established:* Developed a computational account of how different classes of scribal variation affect possible genealogical histories and evaluated alternative stemma hypotheses against an empirical variation model, including artificial and historical traditions. *Overlap:* A rigorous antecedent for treating shared variants as genealogical evidence conditioned on how likely those variants are to arise independently. *Boundary:* It evaluates witnesses and textual variations in manuscript traditions. The present classification task has synthetic source-sharing assignments and tests discrimination from endpoint reports, without estimating an entire tradition’s copying graph or a calibrated model of variant inheritance. **Priority: essential for the theory of genealogical evidence.** URL: https://doi.org/10.1093/llc/fqt032

**Hoenen (2019), An Open Problem in Computational Stemmatology: A Model for Contamination.** *What it established:* Formalized textual contamination, in which a witness may receive readings from multiple exemplars, using temporally ordered graph structures rather than restricting copying histories to trees. *Overlap:* The Y and synthesis constructions make mixed or shared inputs central, so a graph-based tradition with multiple predecessors is a more appropriate comparison than tree reconstruction alone. *Boundary:* The current experiments do not identify the direction or particular intermediate nodes of such a graph. The “Y” output pattern in the experiment should not be equated with historical contamination, because the Y consists of one shared-root pair and a separate report, while Hoenen’s case may involve one witness drawing readings from multiple texts. **Priority: high for bounding genealogical claims.** URL: https://doi.org/10.6092/issn.2532-8816/8555

**McCollum and Turnbull (2024), Using Bayesian Phylogenetics to Infer Manuscript Transmission History.** *What it established:* Adapted probabilistic transmission models to text-critical collations, including variation-aware substitutions, and provided a workflow connecting TEI XML collation to Bayesian phylogenetic inference. *Overlap:* Offers a richer account of uncertainty in textual transmission and lineage hypotheses than mere shared n-gram overlap. *Boundary:* It assumes collated readings from textual witnesses and estimates latent history under an explicit transmission model, unlike the current discriminative classifier with controlled ground-truth source-sharing classes and no direct ancestor text. **Priority: medium, useful to prevent overclaiming novelty relative to probabilistic genealogy.** URL: https://doi.org/10.1093/llc/fqad089

## Manuscript-ready positioning paragraph (analytical draft, not a claim of first discovery)

Text provenance has been studied through at least three different observation models. Source-tracing benchmarks link generated claims to available upstream documents (Zhu et al., 2025), while near-duplicate, plagiarism, and stemmatic methods use overlap or shared variants to detect and model dependence (Broder et al., 1997; Barrón-Cedeño et al., 2010; Andrews & Macé, 2013; Hoenen, 2019). Work on generated-text detection and authorship attribution identifies distributional traces of a producing model, sometimes without labeled training data, and watermarking methods deliberately insert detectable signals (Uchendu et al., 2020; Mitchell et al., 2023; Kirchenbauer et al., 2023). More directly, recent agent-provenance work reconstructs interactions either from generation-time keyed signals or from accessible prior turns (Nian et al., 2026; Tang et al., 2026). Our experiment isolates a different observation condition: the predictor receives uninstrumented endpoint reports about a common constructed event, with their upstream observations and assignments withheld. The question is whether cross-report relations improve recovery of experimentally assigned shared ancestry beyond the information in the reports' individual features taken together. That claim depends on the matched-assignment design and comparative estimator, not on the novelty of n-gram overlap or the assumption that lexical similarity uniquely identifies a copied ancestor.

## TACL rules verified November 1, 2026

**July 9, 2026 review-delay notice:** “Important Update on TACL Review Timelines”, https://transacl.org/ojs/index.php/tacl/announcement/view/121 . The editors state that increased submissions, reviewer demand, and summer schedules may extend reviews. Do not budget a guaranteed two-month first outcome using the legacy target timeline.

**Appendix policy:** “New TACL Appendices Policy”, February 16, 2024, effective March 1, 2024, https://transacl.org/index.php/tacl/announcement/view/105 . Main original submission retains a **ten-page content** limit. **Category 1, up to five pages:** reproducibility details, preprocessing, features, prompts, parameters, annotation directions and necessary derivations. **Category 2, up to three pages:** complementary figures and tables explicitly referenced in the main article. Other discursive free text is restricted. Appendices follow references in the *same PDF*, are anonymous, and are not reviewed. The journal may reject or return overlength appendices. These permitted in-PDF appendices are different from **external supplemental submissions**, which the author guidelines prohibit.

**Preprints and anonymity:** https://transacl.org/ojs/index.php/tacl/about/submissions . TACL treats arXiv and non-archival workshop versions as non-archival. An earlier public preprint can coexist with submission, with a fully anonymized submitted paper. In confidential “Comments to the Editor,” disclose the server or workshop, preprint title, URL, and initial posting date. Do not refer to the overlapping preprint in the anonymized submitted manuscript. Public versions may identify authors under the ACL policy, but publicity can compromise practical blinding. TACL bans simultaneous review or archival publication of substantially overlapping material elsewhere. The prior rejected **npj Complexity** submission is a different journal process, not the special nine-month bar for ACL/ARR-conference-reviewed papers, but prior-overlap facts still deserve ordinary accuracy in editorial disclosure.

**Code and data under double blind:** The submission instructions explicitly prohibit **links to external supplementary material, even anonymized URLs**, because tracking analytics could expose reviewers. That includes a review-time GitHub/GitLab code-repository link offered as supplementary material. Authors should instead state anonymously whether and how code/data will be made available. Separately, the official appendix rules permit URLs when necessary for exact replication within Category 1, so the conservative resolution is to keep externally hosted **review-time supplementary code/data links out of the PDF**, and use the limited in-PDF appendix for instructions/examples. If a particular citation is essential, it may warrant a question to the editors rather than unilateral reinterpretation of the two policies. The author guidelines positively encourage code/data release, but disclosure of an intended release does not itself authorize public release of this project's materials.

**Formatting:** https://transacl.org/tacl-submission-templates/tacl2021v1-submission-formatting-instructions.pdf . Check anonymous PDF metadata, self-citation in third person, figures and main text limits, and the LaTeX template before a final submission build.
